    \begin{parts}
        \part \textbf{Solve for Nash Equilibria.}
        Set $v=1/2$ and subtract $1/2$ from all entries. The resulting game is zero-sum. 
        \begin{table}[h!]
        \centering
        \begin{tabular}{c|ccc}
        Commander 1 $\backslash$ Commander 2 & Attack & Defend & Grow \\ \hline
        Attack & $(0,\ 0)$ & $(-1/2,\ 1/2)$ & $(1/2,\ -1/2)$ \\
        Defend & $(1/2,\ -1/2)$ & $(0,\ 0)$ & $(-1/2,\ 1/2)$ \\
        Grow & $(-1/2,\ 1/2)$ & $(1/2,\ -1/2)$ & $(0,\ 0)$ \\
        \end{tabular}
    \end{table}
        We now have that $u_1(_1, s_2) = -u_2(s_1, s_2)$. Suppose both commanders adopt a mixed strategy with strategy profile $(\sigma_1,\sigma_2)$. von Neumann's minmax theorem gives for any finite, two-player, zero-sum game
        \begin{align*}
            \max_{\sigma_1}\min_{\sigma_2}u_1(\sigma_1, \sigma_2) = \min_{\sigma_2}\max_{\sigma_1}u_1(\sigma_1,\sigma_2).
        \end{align*}
        A lecture theorem states that a strategy profile $(\sigma_1^*,\sigma_2^*)$ is a Nash Equilibrium iff it is in the argmin of commander 1's problem and argmax of commander 2's.
        The lecture notes give a tractable pair of linear programs that characterize Nash equilibria of this game:
        \begin{align*}
            \max_{\sigma_1,z}\quad&z 
            && &\min_{\sigma_2,z}\quad&z\\
            \text{s.t.}\quad& z\leq\sum_{s_1}\sigma_1(s_1)\cdot u_1(s_1, s_2)\;\forall\;s_2\in S_2
            && &\text{s.t.}\quad& z\geq\sum_{s_2}\sigma_2(s_2)\cdot u_1(s_1, s_2)\;\forall\;s_1\in S_1\\
            &\sum_{s_1}\sigma_1(s_1)=1,\;\sigma_1(s_1) \geq 0\;\forall\;s_1\in S_1
            && &&\sum_{s_2}\sigma_2(s_2)=1,\;\sigma_2(s_2) \geq 0\;\forall\;s_2\in S_2
        \end{align*}
        Where $s_i\in\{\text{Attack, Defend, Grow}\}$ and $u_1(\cdot)$ is drawn from the table above. This is instantiated in the accompanying code. The Nash Equilibrium is
        \begin{align*}
            \sigma_1(\text{Attack, Defend, Grow}) = \sigma_2(\text{Attack, Defend, Grow}) = (0.\overline{33}, 0.\overline{33}, 0.\overline{33})
        \end{align*}



        \part \textbf{Prove Nash Equilibrium.}
        Set $v = 0$. 
        Suffice to verify that $\sigma^*_i = (1/3,1/3,1/3)$ is a best response to $\sigma^*_{-i} = (1/3,1/3,1/3)$. We are told this NE is unique so we do not need to search and check other candidates. Since the payoffs are symmetric for each commander and combination of actions, we can apply the argument for Commander 1 to Commander 2. 

        \paragraph{By contradiction.} Consider the expected payoff of Commander 1. Suppose $\exists\;\hat\sigma_1\neq \sigma^*_1$ with strictly higher expected payoffs under Commander 2's strategy $\sigma_2^* = (1/3,1/3,1/3)$. 
        
        Note for general $\sigma_1$ we have
        \begin{align*}
            E_{s_1\sim\sigma_1}\big[E_{s_2\sim\sigma_2^*}[u_1(s_1,s_2)]\big] &= 
            E_{s_1\sim\sigma_1}\big[1/3 \left(u_1(s_1,A) + u_1(s_1,D) + u_1(s_1,G)\right)\big]\\
            &= \sigma_1(A)\cdot 1/3 \left(0 + 0 + 1\right) + \sigma_1(D)\cdot 1/3 \left(1 + 0 + 0\right) + \sigma_1(G)\cdot 1/3 \left(0 + 1 + 0\right)\\
            &= 1/3 (\sigma_1(A) + \sigma_1(D) +\sigma_1(G)).
        \end{align*}
        
        Then under our supposition
        \begin{align*}
            E_{s_1\sim\hat\sigma_1}\big[E_{s_2\sim\sigma_2^*}[u_1(s_1,s_2)]\big] & > E_{s_1\sim\sigma^*_1}\big[E_{s_2\sim\sigma_2^*}[u_1(s_1,s_2)]\big]\\
            1/3 (\hat\sigma_1(A) + \hat\sigma_1(D) +\hat\sigma_1(G)) &> 1/3 (1/3 + 1/3 + 1/3) = 1/3.
        \end{align*}
        But for any $\sigma_1$, $\sum_{s_1}\sigma_1(s_1) = 1$ so we have $\hat\sigma_1(A) + \hat\sigma_1(D) +\hat\sigma_1(G) = 1$ and $1/3 > 1/3$, a contradiction.

        \part \textbf{Experiment I convergence.}
        Yes, in Experiment I fictitious play converges in the time-average sense to the Nash Equilibrium found in part (a) with $\sigma_i = (1/3,1/3,1/3)$.
        And yes this would hold for any valid initial histories $(\eta^0_1,\eta^0_2)$.
        This is given by lecture theorem [Robinson 51], which states that fictitious play converges in the time-average sense to a Nash Equilibrium of the game $G$, for any initial beliefs, if $G$ is a two-player zero-sum game.
        In part (a) we subtracted $1/2$ from all payoffs to get a two-player zero-sum game, and we are given in part (a) that this preserves the Nash equilibria.
        The time averages in Experiment I are therefore the same as in the zero-sum game, and they converge to a Nash Equilibrium.

        \part \textbf{Experiment II and III convergence.}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item \emph{Experiment II does not converge:} With $v=0$, the payoff matrix is the same payoff matrix as the modified rock-paper-scissors payoff matrix used by Shapley to prove that fictitious play does not converge. 
            In his game, the first action profile was off-diagonal, which is also the conditions for Experiment II. 
            From lecture: Shapley showed that play cycles among 6 (off-diagonal) profiles with periods of ever-increasing length, thus non-convergence.
            \item \emph{Experiment III does converge:} The game is symmetric, the histories are identical, and the tie-break rule is the same. In each stage of play, both commanders choose the same action and their histories stay equal. 
            Each commander's best response continues in a deterministic cycle of $A,D,D,G,G,A$. 
            At the start, and after one complete block, all fractions are the same and tie-breaking goes to action $A$ for both commanders. 
            Then beliefs lead expected payoffs to be highest for $D$ for two iterations (the second through tie-break rules). Following this, expected payoffs are highest for $G$ for two iterations before tie-break rules lead to a best response of $A$.
            Each action is played twice in every 6 stages, so in a time-average sense the empirical proportions converge to the mixed Nash Equilibrium from part (b). We use the same logic here as was presented in lecture for the heads/tails example that converges.  
        \end{itemize}

    \begin{parts}
        \part \textbf{The model and its rest points.}
        \begin{subparts}
            \subpart \emph{Expected costs and regret.} 
            \begin{align*}
                c(S) < c(L) \iff 8 + 20q < 13 \iff 20q < 5 \iff q < 1/4
            \end{align*}
            From this, we know that the optimal route for $q=0.05$ is $S$ and for $q=0.35$ is $L$.

            We define the regret of trip $i$ under probability $q$ and optimal choice $i^*$ as
            \begin{align*}
                \operatorname{Regret}^{(i)}_q = E[c(i)] - E[c(i^*)]
            \end{align*}
            So we have
            \begin{align*}
                \operatorname{Regret}^{(S)}_{q=0.05} = 0,\quad
                \operatorname{Regret}^{(L)}_{q=0.05} = 4,\qquad\text{and}\qquad
                \operatorname{Regret}^{(S)}_{q=0.35} = 2, \quad
                \operatorname{Regret}^{(L)}_{q=0.35} = 0.
            \end{align*}

            \subpart \emph{Pessimistic operator.}
            The pessimistic operator has belief Beta$(2,4)$ with mean $\hat q = 1/3$. From part (i), route $L$ has lower expected cost under this belief. In a myopic strategy the operator then sails $L$. Since the operator sees only what happens on the route it sails, it cannot update its belief after this trip, but continues to make the same myopic decision where lower expected costs $L$. We call this a trap. 

            \subpart \emph{Self confirming equilibria.}
            A pair $(\sigma, \hat{\mathcal Q})$ is a self confirming equilibrium if i) every action is optimal under its prediction and ii) predictions are correct for every action used. 

            The operator only observes hits on $S$, so ii) requires $\hat q = q$ whenever $\sigma > 0$, but otherwise does not place a restriction on $\hat q$. 
            \begin{itemize}
                    \item ($q=0.05$) If $\sigma > 0$, a correct prediction requires $\hat q = 0.05$. Under this prediction the best response is $\sigma = 1$. If $\sigma = 0$, the prediction is never tested, and $\sigma = 0$ is optimal for any $\hat q \geq 1/4$. The self-confirming equilibria are $(1, 0.05)$ and $(0,\hat q)$ for all $\hat q \geq 1/4$.
                    \item ($q=0.35$) If $\sigma > 0$, a correct prediction requires $\hat q = 0.35$. Under this prediction the best response is $\sigma = 0$, a contradiction. If $\sigma = 0$, the prediction is never tested, and $\sigma = 0$ is optimal for any $\hat q \geq 1/4$. The self-confirming equilibria are $(0,\hat q)$ for all $\hat q \geq 1/4$.
            \end{itemize}
            No equilibria use both routes through the above logic ($\sigma \notin (0,1)$).
        \end{subparts}


        \part \textbf{The trap.}
        \begin{subparts}
            \subpart \emph{Trapping frontier.} We know the expectation of a beta distribution is $\hat q = \alpha / (\alpha + \beta)$. After $n$ $S$ trips with $h$ hits, the posterior Beta's expectation is $\hat q_n = (\alpha + h) / (\alpha + \beta + n)$. 

            From part (a), we also know that whenever $\hat q \geq 1/4$, the operator sails $L$ for good. 

            Therefore, the operator sails $L$ for good when 
            \begin{align*}
                8 + 20 \cdot \frac{1 + h}{6 + n} &\geq 13 \tag{setting $\alpha=1,\beta=5$; sailing $L$ on tie-break}\\
                h&\geq \tfrac14\cdot (6 + n) - 1
            \end{align*}
            The smallest whole number of hits that make a permanent switch to $L$ for various $n$ are
            \begin{align*}
                n=1:\quad& h\geq \left\lceil\tfrac14\cdot (6 + 1) - 1\right\rceil = \left\lceil\tfrac34\right\rceil = 1\\
                n=2:\quad& h\geq \left\lceil\tfrac14\cdot (6 + 2) - 1\right\rceil = \left\lceil1\right\rceil = 1\\
                n=3:\quad& h\geq \left\lceil\tfrac14\cdot (6 + 3) - 1\right\rceil = \left\lceil\tfrac54\right\rceil = 2\\
                n=6:\quad& h\geq \left\lceil\tfrac14\cdot (6 + 6) - 1\right\rceil = \left\lceil2\right\rceil = 2
            \end{align*}
            \subpart \emph{Trapping probability in first two trips.}
            Suffices to see in subpart (i) that the smallest whole number of hits that make a permanent switch to L for $n\in \{1,2\}$ is $h\geq 1$. Therefore a hit on either of the first two trips makes the operator switch to $L$. 

            The probability of a hit on either of the first two trips can be written as
            \begin{align*}
                P(\text{hit on trip 1 or 2}) &= P(\text{hit on trip 1}) + P(\text{miss on trip 1 and hit on trip 2})\\
                &= 0.05 + (1-0.05)\cdot 0.05 = 0.0975
            \end{align*}

The operator sails $S$ on trip $m+1$ when $\hat q < 1/4 \implies h < (m-2)/4$.

    \titledquestion{Planning with a model}
    \begin{parts}
        \part \textbf{Two missiles.}
        \paragraph{State space.} Define the state $z$ by the number of missiles remaining of each type $n_t\in\{0,1\}$ and the number of remaining interceptors $s\in\{0,1,2\}$. The stage is the number of remaining salvos $k\in\{0,1,2\}$. Define the action as the number of interceptors assigned to each type $x_t\in\{0,1,2\}$
        \begin{align*}
            z =(n_H, n_M, s) \in \{0,1\}^2\times\{0,1,2\},
            \qquad a = (x_H, x_M) \in \{0,1,2\}^2.
        \end{align*}
        
        \paragraph{Stage 0.} In stage 0, there are $2\times2\times3$ states. No actions can be taken and the value is the negative of the active missile counts times their respective costs:
        \begin{align*}
            V_0(z) = V(n_H,n_M,s) = -\sum_t w_tn_{t} = -(10n_{H} + 3n_{M})\;\forall\;s.
        \end{align*}

        \paragraph{Stage 1.} There are only costs in stage 0 so $c(x,a) = 0$ for $k > 0$. Value in this stage is the value from each action in stage 0, times the likelihood of reaching that state. Assume $\delta=1$.
        \begin{align*}
            V_1(z) &= \max_a\; \sum_{z'}P(z'\mid z,a)\,V_0(z')\\
            &= \max_a\; \sum_{z'}P(z'\mid z,a)\Big(-\sum_t w_t\, n'_t\Big)\\
            &= \max_a\; -\sum_t w_t \sum_{z'}P(z'\mid z,a)\, n'_t\\
            &= \max_{x_t}\;\Big\{ -\sum_t w_t\, n_t\,(1-p_t)^{x_t}\;:\; \sum_{t}x_t \leq s\Big\}\\
            V(n_H, n_M, s) &= -\min_{x_H,x_M}\;\left\{10 \cdot n_{H} \cdot (0.5)^{x_H}+ 3\cdot n_{M}\cdot (0.3)^{x_M} :x_H + x_M \leq s \right\}
        \end{align*}
        Solving for each state $z$ we have
        \begin{align*}
            V_1(n_H,n_M,0) &= -(10\cdot n_H + 3\cdot n_M)\\
            V_1(0,1,1) &= -\min\{3.0, 0.9\} = -0.9\\
            V_1(1,0,1) &= -\min\{5.0, 10.0\} = -5.0\\
            V_1(1,1,1) &= -\min\{8.0, 10.9\} = -8.0\\
            V_1(1,1,2) &= -\min\{5.5, 5.9, 10.27\} = -5.5
        \end{align*}
        Note $V_1(0,0,2),\; V_1(0,0,1),\; V_1(1,0,2),\; V_1(0,1,2)$ are unreachable states in stage 1 since they are states with downed missles but a full stock of interceptors.

        \paragraph{Stage 2.} In stage 2 our state is $z = (1,1,2)$. We have six actions $a\in\{(0,0), (0,1), (1,0), (1,1), (0,2), (2,0)\}$. 
        Running through these actions, we have
        \begin{align*}
            a=(0,0):&\quad V_1(1,1,2) &&= -5.5\\
            a=(1,0):&\quad 0.5\,V_1(0,1,1) + 0.5\,V_1(1,1,1) &&= -4.45\\
            a=(0,1):&\quad 0.7\,V_1(1,0,1) + 0.3\,V_1(1,1,1) &&= -5.9\\
            a=(2,0):&\quad 0.75\,V_1(0,1,0) + 0.25\,V_1(1,1,0) &&= -5.5\\
            a=(1,1):&\quad 0.15\,V_1(0,1,0) + 0.35\,V_1(1,0,0) + 0.15\,V_1(1,1,0) + 0.35\,V_1(0,0,0) &&= -5.9\\
            a=(0,2):&\quad 0.91\,V_1(1,0,0) + 0.09\,V_1(1,1,0) &&= -10.27\\
        \end{align*}
        And $V_2(1,1,2) = -4.45$ with action $a=(1,0)$. The optimal policy is $(1,0)$ in stage 2 and $(0,1)$ in stage 1 if missile $H$ is intercepted else $(1,0)$
        The best way to fire both interceptors at once is $a=(2,0)$, two at the $H$ missile. The optimal policy saves $1.05$ in expectation. 

        \paragraph{Only H.}
        With only missile $H$, the defender's optimal policy is indifferent between firing both interceptors in stage 2, both in stage 1, or one in each. This is from the givens: i) interception probability is independent, ii) there is no value to saved missiles, and iii) the only cost is un-intercepted missiles in stage 0. $V_2(1,2) = -10\;(1-0.5)^2=-2.5$ under each policy. 

        \paragraph{Observing.} Observing helps with H and M because with non-zero probability missile $H$ is intercepted in stage 2, which would allow the defender to divert its remaining interceptor toward missile $M$ and, with a nonzero probability, further reducing its cost. With only missile $H$ there is no other target to defend and no value to remaining interceptors, so there is no additional gain from observing the outcome of its first shot and possibly diverting its second. 

        \part \textbf{The state.}
        From lecture, a valid state is a summary of the history such that any two histories with the same summary give the same expected cost and transition probability, for every action.
        To justify that the number of live missiles of each type, the interceptors left, and the salvos left form a state, I first show they are necessary, then sufficient.
        
        \emph{Necessary.} 
        Costs are assessed after the final salvo and are a function of the number of live missiles of each type. There is no way to calculate the expected cost without this information so it must be in the state.
        The number of interceptors left determines which actions are possible for the defender. There is no way to determine which states are reachable without this information.
        The number of salvos left determines when costs are assessed -- only after the final one. There is no way to assess costs or end the finite-horizon game without this information.
        
        \emph{Sufficient.}
        Costs are calculated only after there are no salvos remaining, on the number of live missiles of each type. This is determined by information in the proposed state.
        Transition probabilities are calculated from the number of live missiles of each type and the action taken. Actions are only limited by the number of remaining interceptors and all strike probabilities are iid by missile type so only the number of remaining interceptors is needed.
        
        Hence the proposed summary of the game is necessary and sufficient for a state.

        \paragraph{Number of states in large raid.}
        There are $1,056=(5+1)\times(10+1)\times(15+1)$ possible combinations of counts of live missiles by type.
        There are $37=36+1$ possible counts of remaining interceptors.
        And there are $4=3+1$ possible remaining salvos.
        Together, the size of the state space is $156,288=1,056\times37\times4$ unique combinations. 

        If instead of the count of missiles by type, each missile is tracked as alive or dead, we'd have $2^{(5+10+15)} = 2^{30}$ possible combinations of missile state leading to a state space that is $148 \times 2^{30}$ unique combinations. 

        If two of the $M$ missiles carried a different probability, then these missile subtypes would need to be counted separately in order to determine the proper transition probabilities. The state space could split the $M$ missile types into type $M1$ and type $M2$ which would have equal costs, but different hit probabilities.

            \subpart \emph{The recursion.}
            Let $n = (n_H, n_M, n_L)$, $a = (x_H, x_M, X_L)$
            We start with the stage after the final salvo (no salvos left),
            \begin{align*}
                V_0(n, s) = 10\;n_H + 3\;n_M + 1\;n_L
            \end{align*}
            Moving one stage back, we get our recursive step. For any $k\in[1,K)$
            \begin{align*}
                V_k(n, s) &= \min_{a} \sum_{n'} P(n' \mid n, a) \, V_{k-1}(n', s') && \text{(best policy)} \\
                V_k(n, s) &= \sum_{n'} P(n' \mid n, a) \, V_{k-1}(n', s'), \quad a = \pi(n, s, k) && \text{(doctrine $\pi$)}
            \end{align*}
            And stage $K$ is the above step, but only at the initial state of the raid. 

            Backward induction gives exact values after $K$ steps because it is a finite horizon game of $K$ steps. This recursive loop is the complete value of the expected cost.

            No, rollout can never do worse than base. A rollout policy is at least as good as base because in each salvo the policy is allowed to scan the value of all one-salvo actions followed by the base policy at the next salvo, and choose the best one. The same comparison holds at every later salvo, so by induction, the cost of the rollout policy is at most the cost of the base from every state.

            The result from lecture only promises that the rollout policy does at least as good as the base policy, because the base policy action is among the set of available choices. There is no guarantee that it does strictly better, this would depend on the base policy and raid. In our rollout calculations, we do see that rollout does strictly better than our selected doctrines on both raids.

            This general iterated rollout method is policy iteration. In every iteration we are defining a new policy: the best one-step action plus the cost to go of the previous policy. At the next iteration, any incremental value added from the current action's step gets incorporated into the cost-to-go. 

            In our finite-horizon game, this iterative approach needs at most $K-1$ rounds. In our final salvo, the optimal policy is the base policy: fire everything now. Moving one salvo back, our rollout allows us to identify an optimal two-salvo policy by taking the optimal one-salvo action followed by fire-everything now. By round $K-1$, we have iterated into the optimal policy and no more improvement can be had. 

            This strategy cannot do better than the best policy over salvo plans by definition. That policy is optimal. Both the rollout and best policies choose the plan from the set \texttt{salvo\_plans}, the latter of which is optimal over every stage.  


            \subpart \emph{Exact vs. expected.} 
            In class the expected cost from rollout of ``equal share, spread'' on the large raid was 8.04 (se=0.09). From part (d.i) we get the exact cost is 7.984. The difference between simulated and exact is less than one standard error. 

            Simulation is a proxy for true transition probabilities. The expected cost from simulation can have error and the plan with the lowest simulated score has an exact score at least as high as the lowest exact score.
            Rollout takes the plan with the lowest expected score and with simulation a simulated lowest score may be lower than the exact lowest score, leading to a suboptimal policy action and higher expected policy cost. 
            
            By the law of large numbers, an infinite number of simulations would recover the true expected cost and the best policy. We could reduce rollout from simulation errors by increasing the number of simulations toward infinity. 
        \end{subparts}
